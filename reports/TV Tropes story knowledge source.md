# Borrow TV Tropes' vocabulary, not its text

Table Read should add storytelling-pattern knowledge, but only as a small vocabulary of TV-native patterns that Table Read writes itself, used in two features the writer triggers. It should not import TV Tropes. TV Tropes is the richest map of television craft anywhere: about **30,000 tropes**, **488,632 TV examples** in a 2020 crawl, dedicated indexes for series structure, and a 25-plus-term vocabulary for *how* a work plays a trope. But all its text since July 2012 is **CC BY-NC-SA 3.0**. Its robots.txt sets `ai-train=no` and blocks the Claude and ChatGPT agents, and Cloudflare turns away every non-browser request. Every dataset built from it is NonCommercial in substance, mostly about film, about six years old, and none labels episodes. Detection is a second wall. On movie synopses the best LLM result is **27.1 F1, against 64.9 for humans**, and planted keywords fool GPT-4 up to **97%** of the time. A "list the tropes in this script" feature would therefore fail Table Read's own grounding gates. What remains freely usable is the trope *names and concepts* (copyright does not protect them), public-domain Polti, the MIT-licensed Literary Theme Ontology, and CC0 Wikidata IDs. Table Read already has two places ready for this knowledge. One is a cliché-avoidance step that the design doc specifies but the build only half-delivers. The other is the Narrative Map's optional, evidence-cited Hero's Journey lens. The decisions below are yours to make before any code is written.

## TV Tropes is a 30,000-trope fan wiki with no data model

TV Tropes organizes knowledge as wiki pages named `Namespace/PageName`, and each page carries an editor-set page type: Trope, Work, Index, Sub-page and so on ([TV Tropes: What Page Types Mean](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Administrivia/WhatPageTypesMean)). A **trope page** opens with a multi-paragraph prose definition. It links related tropes in prose ("Super-Trope of", "Compare", "Not to be confused with") and then lists examples grouped by medium; live-action TV gets its own example subpage on big tropes ([Main/BottleEpisode](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/BottleEpisode)). A **work page** for a series such as *Breaking Bad* gives a summary, then an alphabetical list of "Trope Name: explanation" entries. Big shows split this list across five A–Z subpages and add Recap, Characters, YMMV and Trivia tabs ([Series/BreakingBad](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Series/BreakingBad)). Underneath sits an implicit entity model of trope, work, occurrence, episode and character. Nothing is typed, though, beyond the namespace. Each work–trope occurrence is stored twice, once on the trope page and once on the work page, as free text, and nothing keeps the two copies in sync.

The taxonomy is a loose graph, not a tree. Four "Main Indexes" (Genre, Media, Narrative, Topical) and about 45 other top-level indexes sit above thousands of hand-maintained index pages, and any trope can belong to dozens of them ([Main/Tropes](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/Tropes)). Bottle Episode, for instance, sits under Live-Action TV Tropes, Money Tropes, Restriction Tropes and Plots at once. Some named concepts are indexes rather than tropes: Status Quo Is God is typed as an index page ([Main/StatusQuoIsGod](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/StatusQuoIsGod)). Synonyms resolve through redirects, so "Cold Open" lands on **The Teaser** ([Main/TheTeaser](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/TheTeaser)). Anyone who wants a hierarchy has to rebuild it from index membership, prose relation phrases and redirects.

The site's most transferable idea is its **"Playing With a Trope" vocabulary**: more than 25 named ways a work can treat a trope. Some describe structure (Straight, Inverted, Subverted, Averted, Deconstructed, Implied), some tone (Exaggerated, Downplayed, Played for Laughs or Drama), and some in-story awareness (Lampshaded, Invoked, Defied, Exploited) ([Main/PlayingWithATrope](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/PlayingWithATrope)). In practice these labels appear only as optional free text at the start of an example ("Addressed by the Baby: Subverted…"), never as structured fields ([BreakingBad/TropesAToB](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/BreakingBad/TropesAToB)).

Scale is large and growing, but no official statistics exist. The site had **30,065 tropes in March 2021** and about 250,000 edits a month ([The Believer](https://www.thebeliever.net/logger/tv-trope-orgs-treasure-and-trash/)). A 2020 crawl of film, TV and literature alone found **29,457 tropes and 1,919,844 examples across 40,435 works**. That included **7,921 TV titles using 27,134 distinct tropes** ([Gala et al., arXiv:2011.00092](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.00092)). Definitions average 277 tokens and examples 63 ([same](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.00092)). A live snapshot on 2026-10-11 showed about seven edits a minute, and new Recap pages were still appearing for shows like *House* ([New Articles](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/articles_new.php)).

Quality splits sharply between the two kinds of content. **Definitions are the valuable asset**: careful, spoiler-policed, and often written as genuine craft explanations. **Example lists are where the noise lives.** Policy bans "zero-context" entries and treats tropes as neutral tools ([Zero-Context Example](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Administrivia/ZeroContextExample); [Tropes Are Tools](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Administrivia/TropesAreTools)), but coverage follows fandom size, not importance. In one sample of 256 contributors the median self-reported age was **20, and 64% were male** ([Gala et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.00092)). Researchers who scraped the site describe its data as long-tailed, biased toward popular works, and in need of wrangling ([García-Ortega et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2006.05380)). The register is playful. Pages carry joke captions and joke names such as "Kent Brockman News", so a raw trope name is often a poor label to show a professional writer ([Main/TelevisionTropes](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/TelevisionTropes)).

## Its TV craft coverage is deep but shaped by US network TV

For episodic writers, TV Tropes covers the medium better than any open source. Three indexes do most of the work. **Live-Action TV Tropes** covers the medium itself: Bottle Episode, Recycled Set, Season Finale, a Pilot Episode sub-index ([Main/LiveActionTVTropes](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/LiveActionTVTropes)). **Series Tropes** covers whole-series shape: Myth Arc, Story Arc, Wham Episode, Mid-Season Twist, Early-Installment Weirdness, Cerebus Syndrome, Grand Finale ([Main/SeriesTropes](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/SeriesTropes)). **Television Tropes** covers TV as it appears inside stories. One more page, **Layout of a Season**, maps the architecture of a US network season: a mid-season twist around episode 7 of 13, the "Ep-15 Shift" at February sweeps, and the Back 9. It contrasts all this with British Brevity ([Main/LayoutOfASeason](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/LayoutOfASeason)).

Nearly every structural concept a TV writer uses exists as a page. Those confirmed include The Teaser, Previously On…, Act Break, Commercial Break Cliffhanger, A Day in the Limelight, Clip Show, Multi-Part Episode, Monster of the Week and Will They or Won't They? ([Act Break](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/ActBreak); [Commercial Break Cliffhanger](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/CommercialBreakCliffhanger); [A Day in the Limelight](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/ADayInTheLimelight); [Monster of the Week](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/MonsterOfTheWeek)). One expected name, "Cliffhanger Episode", returns 404; the idea is split across Cliffhanger, Season Finale and Commercial Break Cliffhanger ([404](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/CliffhangerEpisode)). That gap is a reminder that any vocabulary built from TV Tropes inherits its naming choices.

Episode-level depth is the exception, not the rule. Each episode's trope list lives on a Recap page, and on 2026-10-11 the live-action Recap index listed **1,211 series**, against thousands of series pages ([Recap/LiveActionTV](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Recap/LiveActionTV)). Recap policy accepts a synopsis, a trope list, or both, so some recaps have no trope list at all ([TV Tropes Recap index](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/Recap)). For most shows, a trope attaches to the whole series, and the episode is named, if at all, inside the free text. The season-structure material is also openly US-network-centric (sweeps, Front 13, Back 9). Streaming-era and short-order formats get far less detail, and those are the formats many of Table Read's writers work in.

## A NonCommercial licence and a bot wall close the data door

The licence settles most of the question. Every page footer states **CC BY-NC-SA 3.0 Unported**, with "permissions beyond the scope of this license" available from thestaff@tvtropes.org ([TV Tropes ToS capture](https://web.archive.org/web/20261011141358/https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/termsofservice.php)). The site was CC BY-SA until July 2012 and then switched, applying the new licence to earlier contributions too ([Wikipedia: TV Tropes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TV_Tropes)). NonCommercial bars any use "primarily intended for or directed toward commercial advantage" ([CC BY-NC-SA 3.0 legal code §4(c)](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc-sa/3.0/legalcode)). Creative Commons' own guidance says "you should not mine NC-licensed databases or other material" for commercial purposes ([CC FAQ](https://creativecommons.org/faq/)). A paid co-writing tool that displays, stores, retrieves or prompts with TV Tropes text is therefore commercial use. A free tier is very likely commercial too, because CC's test looks at purpose, not at who the user is. Page images are third-party material that TV Tropes hosts under fair use and cannot license to anyone ([About Images and Copyright](https://web.archive.org/web/20260901035038/https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Administrivia/AboutImagesAndCopyright)).

Access is blocked as well as unlicensed. robots.txt opens with "As a condition of accessing this website…" and sets `search=yes, ai-train=no` as an express reservation of rights under EU Directive 2019/790 Art. 4. It fully disallows ClaudeBot, `Claude*`, `anthropic-ai`, GPTBot, `ChatGPT-User`, PerplexityBot, CCBot and about two dozen others ([robots.txt capture](https://web.archive.org/web/20260702033210/https://tvtropes.org/robots.txt)). Blocking agents that act on a user's request (`ChatGPT-User`, `Claude*`) signals that TV Tropes does not want AI assistants fetching its pages live, even though the `ai-input` signal is left unset. Cloudflare enforces all this: on 2026-10-11 every non-browser request, including requests for robots.txt itself, got HTTP 403 with a challenge page ([live attempt](https://tvtropes.org/robots.txt)). The site offers no API, no dumps and no sitemap. A runtime feature that has an LLM fetch TV Tropes pages, or a pipeline that scrapes them, would cut against both the licence and the operator's stated wishes. Getting past the challenge would be deliberate circumvention.

| Activity in Table Read | Verdict | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Use trope names ("Bottle Episode", "Will They or Won't They?") as labels | **May** | Names and short phrases are uncopyrightable; ideas and systems are unprotected ([Circular 33](https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ33.pdf)) |
| Write Table Read's own definitions of those concepts | **May**, if independently worded | Same split between idea and expression ([Circular 33](https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ33.pdf)) |
| Link out to a TV Tropes page | **May** (inference; the ToS is silent on linking) | [ToS](https://web.archive.org/web/20261011141358/https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/termsofservice.php) |
| Display, store, or put TV Tropes text into prompts (RAG) | **May not** without a licence | NC §4(c); [CC FAQ on mining](https://creativecommons.org/faq/) |
| Scrape or bulk-download the site | **May not** in practice | [robots.txt](https://web.archive.org/web/20260702033210/https://tvtropes.org/robots.txt); Cloudflare challenge |
| Copy the whole taxonomy (all names plus index structure) | **Unclear — needs a lawyer** | The licence covers compilations "to the extent… protected" ([legal code §1](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc-sa/3.0/legalcode)) |
| Use pre-July-2012 text via All The Tropes | **May**, with attribution and share-alike | ATT is CC BY-SA and says "You can sell it" ([ATT Copyrights](https://allthetropes.org/wiki/All_The_Tropes:Copyrights)) |
| Buy a commercial licence | **Possible, untested** | The contributor grant lets TV Tropes "authorize others" for commercial use ([Welcome to TV Tropes](https://web.archive.org/web/20261001141728/https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Administrivia/WelcomeToTVTropes)) |

The licensing route deserves a caveat. The contributor grant on the Welcome page is broad and includes sublicensing, but the ToS version is narrower and does not mention sublicensing ([ToS §4](https://web.archive.org/web/20261011141358/https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/termsofservice.php)). No past TV Tropes licensing deal was found, and the site's only visible business is ads, a premium subscription and a mobile app launched in March 2025 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TV_Tropes)). The All The Tropes fork escapes the NC term, but its text is frozen at 2012 plus fork edits, and its share-alike clause would attach to any rewritten text Table Read shipped. Notably, no commercial writing tool the research could verify names or links TV Tropes in its product.

## Every derived dataset is film-first, stale, and NonCommercial

The ready-made TV Tropes data falls into three groups, and none of them solves the problem. **DBTropes**, the 2009–2016 Linked Data wrapper of about 8M triples, is offline: the site and its dump URL return HTTP 502, and the Wayback Machine holds no copy of the dump ([LOD Cloud entry](https://lod-cloud.net/json/dbtropes); [dbtropes.org](http://dbtropes.org/)). The broadest surviving dump is **Gala et al.'s TVTROPES** (UMass, 2020): 30K tropes with descriptions, 1.9M examples with their text, and separate film, TV and literature tables joined to IMDb. It sits in a 650 MB Google Drive file with **no stated licence**, and hobby projects in 2026 still build on it ([GitHub: dhruvilgala/tvtropes](https://github.com/dhruvilgala/tvtropes); [tvtropes-state-space](https://github.com/sh1ftmaker/tvtropes-state-space)). A raw dump of 651,522 pages on Hugging Face carries an Apache-2.0 label that conflicts with TV Tropes' terms ([TvTroper card](https://huggingface.co/datasets/RyokoExtra/TvTroper/blob/main/README.md)). The film benchmarks all cover movies only: TiMoS (5,623 synopses × 95 tropes), TiM (684 movies) and CHATTER (88,124 character–trope pairs over 660 screenplays) ([TiMoS](https://github.com/htsucml/TiMoS); [TiM](https://github.com/ander1119/TiM); [CHATTER](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.05227v2)). The only TV Tropes-derived artifact plausibly free of TV Tropes text is a CC0 table mapping about 10K film slugs to IMDb IDs ([tvtropes2imdb](https://huggingface.co/datasets/adorkin/tvtropes2imdb/raw/main/README.md)).

The verdict for a product is blunt. Whatever badge a dataset wears (CC0, Apache, CC BY-SA), its TV Tropes text inherits NonCommercial terms. Nothing has been refreshed since 2023. **No dataset labels tropes at the episode level for TV**; Gala's `tv_tropes` table attaches tropes to whole series. The closest annotated episode-level TV resource labels themes, not tropes: the Literary Theme Ontology benchmark, with 644 episode summaries across 16 series ([JOHD 2026](https://openhumanitiesdata.metajnl.com/articles/10.5334/johd.480)).

### Models know trope names but detect them at about 27 F1

The deeper obstacle is that detection does not work well enough to put in front of professionals. On TiMoS movie synopses, supervised models reach **23.97–25.00 F1 against 64.87 for humans** ([TiMoS, arXiv:2101.07632](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2101.07632)). GPT-4 asked to list tropes from a full multi-label set scores 15.33 F1. Asking about **one trope at a time** lifts it to **27.1**, the best LLM result reported ([Su et al., arXiv:2409.14324](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.14324v1)). The same study found failures that matter for a writing tool. Inserting trope keywords without the actual trope fooled GPT-4 **63–97%** of the time. In **13 of 30** correct answers, the chain-of-thought reasoning behind them was wrong; ordinary parent–child arguments became "Abusive Parents". Adding definitions to ChatGPT's prompt *lowered* F1 from 13.19 to 9.93. The hardest tropes are abstract, implicit or spread across distant scenes, and setup-and-payoff patterns like Chekhov's Gunman and Brick Joke are exactly that kind ([same](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.14324v1)). That is precisely the structure a co-writing tool cares about.

Models clearly *know* TV Tropes. On CHATTER, Gemini-1.5 with no screenplay at all scored **81.9%** at attributing tropes to famous film characters. That beat the best result *with* the screenplay (73.9%), which means the score reflects memory, not reading ([CHATTER](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.05227v2)). That memory is useless for Table Read, whose input is unpublished scripts nobody has catalogued. It also creates two risks. Models will produce plausible TV Tropes-style names that may not exist (no study has measured how often), and they may reproduce memorised TV Tropes prose. No benchmark tests trope detection on TV scripts, and no 2025–2026 frontier-model results exist. Today's models may do better than these numbers, but nobody has shown it.

## Open sources cover themes and plots, not TV devices

No open source replaces TV Tropes' breadth of TV-specific devices, but several cover pieces of it cleanly. The **Literary Theme Ontology** stands out. It has about **3,001 hierarchically organized themes** with roughly 22-word definitions, plus 4,399 annotated stories and 58,898 weighted story–theme annotations, under the **MIT licence** (story descriptions excepted, which are CC BY-SA) ([themeontology.org](https://www.themeontology.org/); [GitHub](https://github.com/theme-ontology/theming)). It is also unusually rich in TV: 41 television collections, covering every Star Trek series, four Twilight Zones, Black Mirror, Columbo, Sherlock, Game of Thrones and Murderbot ([GitHub listing](https://api.github.com/repos/theme-ontology/theming/contents/notes/stories/television)). It describes *what a story is about*, not *how it is built*, and it skews toward science fiction and anthology shows.

Wikidata is thin as content: only **5,265 narrative-motif statements and 3,508 character-type statements** as of 2026-10-11, and no "uses trope" property at all ([Wikidata P6962](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Property:P6962); [WikiProject Narration](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Wikidata:WikiProject_Narration)). It does provide stable CC0 identifiers for concepts like cliffhanger and MacGuffin, plus a TV Tropes ID property (P6839) that can point to a TV Tropes page without bringing in any of its text ([P6839](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Property:P6839)). The TV-structure layer (teaser, act-out, tag, A/B/C runners, the button) has **no open structured dataset anywhere**. These conventions are uncopyrightable formats ([Circular 33](https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ33.pdf)), which means Table Read can and must write that layer itself. Copyright protects only original selection and arrangement in a compilation, not the underlying facts ([Feist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feist_Publications,_Inc.,_v._Rural_Telephone_Service_Co.)), so a hand-curated set of TV patterns with their own wording and IDs stands on solid ground.

## Table Read already has two places built for this

The design doc already asks for trope knowledge. Its narrative components include **cliché avoidance**: "before planning, the model lists well-known stories/tropes to avoid, kept in context during generation" ([design doc §3.2](../docs/narrative-sidekick-design-doc.md)). The build delivers half of that. `storiesToAvoid` exists in the contract, but the writer types it into a free-text field in the Idea wizard, and **only `generate_scene_skeleton` reads it** (`apps/api/drama_manager/scene_skeleton.py:152`). Next Beat candidates, the product's most-used generation path, never see it ([prompt spec §1](../docs/tableread-prompt-spec.md)). A repo-wide grep finds no other mention of "trope" in code or docs. CLAUDE.md asks for a flag when the build and the docs diverge, and this gap needs your decision before any trope work starts.

The second place is the **Narrative Map's optional Hero's Journey lens**. The model sets `applies: false` for ensemble, procedural or anti-hero material. Every stage cites a beat ID, the server attaches the exact quote, an independent verifier rejects unsupported claims, and "the product never forces a 12-stage reading" ([narrative-map spec](../docs/superpowers/specs/2026-07-23-narrative-map-design.md); [prompt spec §5d](../docs/tableread-prompt-spec.md)). That trust model maps directly onto a pattern lens. The patterns become a closed list, the play-type vocabulary answers "how is it used here", and an empty result is never a defect.

Five existing principles constrain what fits. **The Western-canon ban** ("Do not include Western canon story examples in any prompt") rules out TV Tropes-style example lists in prompts even if they were licensed; definitions without examples are the only compliant form ([prompt spec §0](../docs/tableread-prompt-spec.md); [design doc §6.4](../docs/narrative-sidekick-design-doc.md)). **The AI stays quiet in Write mode** (hard rule 8), so nothing can run without a gesture. That includes the `scan_consistency` pass, which runs after every apply, so a cliché check must not ride on it. **"Relationships are not labels but dynamics"** ([design doc §3.2](../docs/narrative-sidekick-design-doc.md)) cuts against trope tags on characters. **The story overview already bans film/Save the Cat labels** in favor of TV-native ones ([prompt spec](../docs/tableread-prompt-spec.md)), so every pattern must pass an "is this TV-native?" test. And **anything the model produces must never be fabricated or presented as fact** (hard rule 9). A pattern claim must cite evidence or not appear.

The market and the audience point the same way. Competitors ship patterns as **templates, not detection**. Plottr's Romance Roadmap Pack attaches archetypes and per-beat "Traps (common mistakes)" to each trope template ([Plottr](https://plottr.com/?p=58158)). Save the Cat offers a TV-episode beat-sheet variant ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/id1495073895)). Only AI coverage services like ScriptReader.ai claim trope detection, and none publishes evidence of its quality ([ScriptReader.ai](https://scriptreader.ai)). Prior research supports trope *suggestion* over trope *judgment*: participants in Adobe and Stanford's TaleStream study rated its trope suggestions more useful than random tropes **97% of the time** ([TaleStream, UIST 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.03790)).

Professional sentiment is the sharpest constraint. Scriptnotes hosts John August and Craig Mazin call AI coverage sycophantic and without taste ([No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/scriptnotes-ai-script-coverage)). FilmDaft finds AI "rewards familiarity" ([FilmDaft](https://filmdaft.com/ai-script-analysis/)). Dramatica's Narrative First argues that TV Tropes makes storytelling "stock and commonplace" ([Narrative First](https://narrativefirst.com/articles/unraveling-the-uselessness-of-the-trope)). Craft educators, meanwhile, frame tropes as tools to establish and then subvert, "the same but different" ([Final Draft blog](https://www.finaldraft.com/blog/using-film-tropes-in-screenwriting)). Features framed as verdicts ("this is a cliché") carry the most risk with Table Read's audience. Features framed as the writer's own lens, or as options to depart from, fit both the craft discussion and Table Read's formative-AI stance. One gap remains: the repo holds **no user research** showing that TV writers want trope labels at all.

## Recommendation: a house vocabulary, two writer-triggered features, no TV Tropes text

**Build a small, closed, house-written vocabulary of TV-native story patterns.** Start with on the order of 100–200 entries across four kinds: structure slots (teaser, act-out, tag, runner), episode forms (bottle, limelight, two-parter, clip show), serial devices (myth arc, wham episode, mid-season twist, will-they-won't-they), and character and relationship patterns. Give each entry a kebab ID, a plain-language name (never TV Tropes' joke titles), and a one- or two-sentence definition written in-house with no example works. Add optional crosswalk fields for Wikidata QIDs, Polti situations and LTO themes. Next to the vocabulary, adopt a plain **"how it's used" field** (straight, subverted, inverted, averted, lampshaded, deconstructed, downplayed, exaggerated) and a separate tone axis; the vocabulary is an idea and the labels are short phrases. TV Tropes stays a **reference for people**: staff may read it to decide which concepts exist, which exercises no licensed right. It is never ingested, scraped, retrieved or put into prompts.

Use that vocabulary in two features, in this order. **First, finish cliché avoidance.** Route the writer's `storiesToAvoid` into the beat-candidate and regenerate bundles. If the model may propose additions, have them arrive as amber, pending items the writer accepts, never injected silently. Gate the change on an eval that checks whether naming a trope in an "avoid" block actually primes the model toward it, because nobody has tested that. **Second, add a patterns lens to the Narrative Map**, inheriting the Hero's Journey trust model: an empty state that is never a defect, a beat citation for every claim, and an independent verifier. Add three safeguards specific to patterns: a closed list, so no invented names can appear; one-pattern-at-a-time verification, the setting where LLMs scored best; and an adversarial "trope-bait" eval with a precision-first bar that mirrors the map's hard grounding gate of 1.0. Plan-mode "same but different" chips are a reasonable third step once the lens shows that detection is good enough. A reference library can wait for the v3 education mode. A cliché judge or overuse score should not be built.

## Conclusion

TV Tropes is most useful to Table Read as evidence of what a TV-native pattern vocabulary should contain, not as data. Its value for writers sits in its TV craft definitions and one excellent idea, the "how it's played" axis. Its 1.9 million fan-written examples are both legally out of reach and the noisiest part of the site. That turns a data-acquisition problem into an editorial one the team fully controls, with no licensing negotiation and no scraping project.

The research also reframes the risk. The main danger is not copyright but the trust failure of confident, unverifiable trope verdicts on a writer's unpublished script. Today's models score about 27 F1 at spotting tropes in new text, and their apparent skill on famous works is memory. Table Read's evidence-and-verifier machinery is the competitive advantage here. Applied to a closed vocabulary, it lets Table Read offer what no competitor demonstrates: pattern claims a writer can check against their own beats, and an honest "nothing notable" when that is the truth.
